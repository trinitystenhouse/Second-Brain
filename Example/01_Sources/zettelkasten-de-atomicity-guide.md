## Summary

A treatment of atomicity: the practice of organising knowledge into discrete single-unit notes, on the principle that knowledge is made of building blocks and a note should contain exactly one of them. The guide is more precise than most about what a building block actually is, naming six types — a concept, an argument, a counter-argument, a model, a hypothesis or theory, and an empirical observation. That taxonomy is more useful than the vaguer "one idea per note" phrasing, because it gives you something to check a note against.

Three reasons are given for bothering. Atomicity is what makes linking possible at all, since connecting ideas requires clear boundaries between them. It sharpens thinking, by forcing engagement with the structure of what you are claiming rather than the flow of the prose. And it serves your future self, who will want to reuse one idea without dragging four others along with it.

The guide explicitly refuses to give a word count, which is the part most summaries of it miss. Atomicity is a compass rather than a rule, and idea atoms come in genuinely different sizes — the analogy offered is hydrogen against plutonium: both atomic, wildly different in scale. The practical method is to write freely first, then test the result with heuristics (is it easy to title? is it understandable at a glance?), then identify which building block it actually contains.

## Key takeaways

- Six knowledge building blocks: concept, argument, counter-argument, model, hypothesis or theory, empirical observation. One per note.
- No fixed size. A note is atomic when it holds one building block, whether that takes forty words or four hundred.
- The title test is the most useful heuristic in practice: a note you cannot title without "and" is usually two notes.
- Atomicity is a precondition for linking, not an alternative filing style. Without clear boundaries there is nothing to connect.
- Write first, atomise second. Trying to be atomic while drafting mostly stops you drafting.

## Relevance to you

This is the standard the `kg-ingest` skill applies when it un-bundles something you have dropped in an inbox. It is why a dense 3,000-word export becomes two to four notes rather than one, and why a single long note gets split even when the content is good — length is not the problem, boundaries are. The title test is worth remembering when you write notes by hand: if you are struggling to name it, you have not finished thinking about it.
